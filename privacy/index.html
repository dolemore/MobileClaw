Privacy Policy — MobileClaw AI Private Launcher

Last updated: 14 November 2026

This privacy policy covers the Android app MobileClaw AI Private Launcher (package name com.la1ncher), published on Google Play by the developer mkry1. The app was called MobileClaw: AI Launcher, Laincher and L.a1.ncher before; it is the same app, and this policy covers it under every name.

Contact: codevibing2.0@outlook.com

MobileClaw is an Android home screen (launcher) with a built-in assistant. It learns how you use your phone on the phone itself. The developer runs no server for the app and receives none of your data. There are no ads, no advertising ID, no analytics SDKs and no trackers.

What the app reads, and where it stays

  • App usage (which app, when): to learn your habits, suggest automations and run "when this app opens" automations. Stays on the phone.
  • Location: to learn the places you return to, notice when you arrive, run place-based automations and show the weather. Stays on the phone, except for place names, weather and nearby places, described below.
  • Notifications you receive: to sort what matters, spot bills, warn you when one may need a look, and build briefings. Stays on the phone.
  • Calendar: for briefings, countdowns and your next event on the home screen. Read when needed, on the phone.
  • Contacts (name and number): to call or message someone when you ask. Read when needed, on the phone.
  • Microphone: for voice commands, only after you tap the microphone. See "Voice input" below.
  • Camera: for the dashcam, only after you start it from its screen, or switch on “Auto record” in the driving screen, which starts it once the car moves and only while that screen is open. No sound is recorded. Clips stay on the phone, the oldest deleted once they pass about 2 GB, and leave it only when you save one to your gallery.
  • Bluetooth: to use the phone as a remote for a TV you pair it with. A direct connection; nothing is stored.
  • What you tell the assistant (things to remember, your answers to its questions): your personal memory ("Second Brain") and getting to know you. Stays on the phone.

When data leaves your phone

Only in these cases, each of which you can avoid:

  1. A cloud AI you connect (optional). If you add your own API key for a cloud AI provider (for example Google Gemini, OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Groq, OpenRouter, Hugging Face or Alibaba Cloud's Qwen), your requests and the information needed to answer them are sent to that provider, under that provider's privacy policy: for example the current time, a summary of your routine, pending bills, or the text of notifications and calendar events when your request is about them. In Settings you choose how much of the AI's work may leave the phone; at 0%, or without a key, nothing is sent.
  2. Getting to know you (optional, asks first). If you agree, and a cloud AI is connected and allowed, once a day a summary of what the phone has learned goes to that AI: which apps you use and when, when your day starts and ends, how many notifications come from which apps, when you charge and when calls happen, the places you go and how long you stay there under the names you gave them (no addresses or coordinates), the names of your automations, and what you told the assistant about yourself when it asked. No message text, no contact names, no saved notes. The app shows you exactly what is sent, and you can turn this off at any time.
  3. Place names. When you spend time somewhere new, the app asks your phone's address lookup service (Android's Geocoder, which on most phones is provided by Google) for the name of that spot, so it can ask you about it by name. The coordinates of that one spot are sent to that service. The name is kept on the phone.
  4. Weather. Your position, rounded to about 1 km, is sent to Open-Meteo, a weather service that needs no account, to get the forecast.
  5. Voice input. When you tap the microphone, your phone's speech recognition service (for example Google's) turns your speech into text, under that service's privacy policy.
  6. On-device AI models (optional). Models you choose to download come from Hugging Face. Like any download, the request shows your IP address to Hugging Face; nothing else about you is sent.
  7. Telegram (optional). If you add your own bot token, the messages between you and your bot go through Telegram's Bot API.
  8. Home Assistant (optional). Commands go to the Home Assistant address you enter, usually on your own network.
  9. MCP servers (optional, off by default). If you add an MCP server's address, the assistant can use that server's tools while it works with a cloud AI you connected. When it uses a tool, what that tool needs goes to that server, under its operator's privacy policy: for example a search term, or text you asked it to save or send. Any tool the server does not mark as read-only asks you first. The assistant does not use MCP servers when it runs on the phone's own model or by itself.
  10. Web content you ask for. The app fetches the RSS feeds you add, the web pages you ask the assistant to read, and the web addresses you put into your own automations.
  11. Nearby places, earthquakes, air quality and exchange rates you ask about. When you ask for the nearest pharmacy, bus stop or similar, your position, rounded to about 100 metres, goes to OpenStreetMap's public Overpass servers (overpass-api.de, overpass.private.coffee) and, if they do not answer, to Photon (photon.komoot.io). Air quality and pollen come from Open-Meteo, with your position rounded to about 1 km. When you ask about earthquakes, the app reads Kandilli's feed through a public service (api.orhanaydogdu.com.tr) or USGS, which gets an area of several hundred kilometres around you in whole degrees. Exchange rates come from Frankfurter and CoinGecko, which get nothing about you beyond the IP address any request shows.
  12. An approximate place from your IP address. When you ask for the weather, nearby places, air quality or earthquakes and the phone does not know where it is, the app asks ipwho.is or ipapi.co where your IP address is, and asking for your network details does the same. These services see your IP address. The home screen, the lock screen and the morning summary never do this.
  13. Things you look up by name. When you ask the app to translate or define a word, look up a food or a barcode, find a song's lyrics or see the next public holidays, what you typed goes to the service that answers it: MyMemory (api.mymemory.translated.net) and Free Dictionary (dictionaryapi.dev), Open Food Facts, LRCLIB and Apple's iTunes search, and Nager.Date, which gets only the country code.
  14. The map and directions on the driving screen. While that screen is open, map pieces for the area around you come from OpenFreeMap (tiles.openfreemap.org), which needs no account and sees your IP address and which area is asked for. When you ask that screen for directions, the place you type or say goes to Nominatim, which the OpenStreetMap Foundation runs (nominatim.openstreetmap.org), and to Photon (photon.komoot.io), each with your position rounded to about 1 km, and where you are and where you are going go to the routing server FOSSGIS runs for OpenStreetMap (routing.openstreetmap.de), again if you leave the route. None of them needs an account.
  15. Local network access (optional, off by default). Devices on your own network can reach the app only with a key shown on the phone.

Storage and security

  • Everything the app learns lives in a local database in the app's private storage.
  • Secrets (AI keys, bot tokens, smart-home and MCP server tokens) are stored with Android's EncryptedSharedPreferences.
  • Uninstalling the app deletes all of it.

Your control and deletion

  • The "What I know about you" screen lists what the app believes about you. Deleting an item deletes the data underneath it, not only its label.
  • Clearing the app's storage, or uninstalling it, erases everything it learned.
  • Every permission is optional. A feature that needs a permission you decline switches off.

Children

The app is meant to be set up by an adult and is not directed at children under 13. A parent can switch a phone to kid mode for a child. In kid mode the app does not learn from the child's use and does not record location. Its study coach and robot friend use the AI on the phone or, when the parent has connected one with their own key, that AI service, under its own privacy policy. What the child writes is checked on the phone first, and nothing about the family goes with it. If the child talks to it, speech goes through the phone's speech recognition service as described under "Voice input".
